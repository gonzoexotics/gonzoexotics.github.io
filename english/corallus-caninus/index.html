Conservation status and documentation

Corallus caninus occurs in northern South America, including Guyana, Suriname, Venezuela and northern Brazil. International trade in the species is regulated by CITES. In husbandry, legal provenance and complete documentation are as important as the animal's condition.

It is important to distinguish Corallus caninus from Corallus batesii. Historically, the trade often grouped both taxa under the name 'Emerald Tree Boa', making documentation and reliable provenance particularly important for captive animals.

SPECIES • BIOLOGY • SIZE

Corallus caninus - Northern Emerald Tree Boa

Corallus caninus is a specialised arboreal constrictor from northern South America. The vivid green of adults, white markings, prehensile tail, large head, heat-sensing organs and exceptionally long anterior teeth equip it as a nocturnal ambush predator.

Important: V11 describes Northern Corallus caninus. The Amazon Basin Emerald Tree Boa is now most commonly recognised as Corallus batesii; it is larger on average, and older 'ETB' recommendations cannot all be transferred automatically to caninus.
SIZEoften about 1.5–1.8 m

The Northern form is usually smaller than large C. batesii.

LIFESTYLEarboreal / nocturnal

Resting in coils and hunting from ambush at night.

REPRODUCTIONviviparous

The female gives birth to live young.

SPECIALISATIONthermoreception

Labial pits detect thermal radiation emitted by prey.

Remarkable teeth

Corallus caninus has exceptionally long anterior teeth for a non-venomous snake. They help it retain prey caught from a branch, but also mean that a bite can cause deep puncture wounds. The absence of venom does not mean the absence of risk.

Colour change

Neonates may be red, orange or yellowish, turning green as they develop. Their resemblance to Morelia viridis is an example of convergent evolution, not close relatedness.

Temperament

It is more accurate to discuss defensiveness and reactivity than aggression. At night, a warm moving object within reach can trigger a rapid strike. Many properly maintained animals are calmer during the day, but individual variation is substantial.

  • the prehensile tail serves as a genuine anchor;
  • the characteristic perch coils distribute weight and allow a rapid transition to hunting;
  • high humidity in natural forest coexists with airflow - a wet, sealed box does not reproduce nature;
  • viviparity distinguishes this species from the egg-laying Morelia viridis.
Basis: Jörg Riethausen and zoological literature; information on C. batesii is not treated as automatically equivalent.
NATURAL HISTORY • RANGE • ECOLOGY

Corallus caninus in the wild

The species is associated with humid forests of northern South America, particularly the Guiana Shield and adjoining northern Amazonia. Depending on the taxonomic treatment, its range includes Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, Venezuela and northern Brazil.

A three-dimensional environment

Living on branches does not mean remaining at one height. The snake uses a network of branches, vines and vegetation; its position determines temperature, humidity, exposure and opportunities for ambush. This underlies Riethausen's emphasis on adequate enclosure height and functional perches.

Hunting strategy

During the day it usually rests in characteristic coils. After dark, it extends the anterior body into a striking position and uses thermoreception to detect warm-blooded prey. It is an ambush predator, not an active pursuit hunter.

Caninus and batesii

Historically treating Amazon Basin and Northern emerald tree boas as one species led to information on size, temperature and biology being mixed. V11 keeps the two taxa separate. This has practical consequences: a protocol for the larger Amazon Basin form is not automatically copied to the Northern form.

The lesson from nature: high humidity cannot be considered without ventilation. Rainforest has enormous air exchange; a closed enclosure that remains constantly wet is a different environment.
ENCLOSURE GUIDE • CORALLUS CANINUS

A Corallus caninus enclosure - designing an arboreal microclimate

For Corallus caninus, the perch is effectively the main living surface, and the enclosure must allow a choice between microclimates. Jörg Riethausen particularly emphasises the height needed for a natural hunting posture and currently uses enclosures approximately 140 cm wide × 100 cm high × 60 cm deep. EmeraldTreeBoas.org describes 122 × 61 × 61 cm as a commonly used minimum, but notes that taller 122 × 61 × 122 cm enclosures substantially increase the depth of the gradient and the choice of behaviour.

COMMONLY USED MINIMUM122 × 61 × 61 cma wide format that requires carefully planned perches
PREMIUM RECOMMENDATION120–140 × 60–90 × 100–120 cma design recommendation combining Riethausen's experience with modern gradient planning
RIETHAUSEN'S EXAMPLE140 × 60 × 100 cmwidth × depth × height; 15 mm Kömacell expanded PVC
Why does height matter? An adult Corallus in a hunting posture may suspend about 60 cm of its body below the highest perch. Riethausen considers around 100 cm of height a practical minimum for this behaviour. However, a very tall enclosure in a cold room may develop an excessive top-to-bottom temperature drop. Height must therefore be planned together with room temperature and the heating system.

1. Enclosure material - what works best?

BEST CHOICE

Expanded PVC / Kömacell

Riethausen builds his enclosures from approximately 15 mm expanded PVC. It insulates well, does not absorb water, and is easy to drill and fit out. EmeraldTreeBoas.org also identifies high-quality PVC as a preferred material for ETBs because of its moisture resistance, environmental stability and ease of disinfection.

A SOUND DIY OPTION

Fully sealed plywood / wood

This can work equally well if every surface and edge is permanently sealed. Properly built wooden enclosures insulate very well, but moisture entering an inadequately sealed area leads to swelling, mould and loss of hygiene.

SECOND CHOICE

Glass

Glass is easy to clean but insulates less well, making it harder to maintain both stable temperature and humidity. Large glass enclosures are heavy. They can work, but PVC allows greater microclimate control and easier perch installation.

2. Gradient design - the most important part of the enclosure

EmeraldTreeBoas.org recommends creating a diagonal gradient: the upper warm side is warmest and usually slightly drier, while the lower cool side is cooler and more humid. The snake can therefore choose not only temperature but also humidity, light and cover.

UPPER LEVEL • WARM SIDE main daytime / digestion perch

This should be high up, but not directly against the ceiling. Its position is determined by measurement: the back of the coiled snake must be within the target temperature range without contact with a panel or lamp.

MIDDLE LEVEL intermediate perch and connecting routes

At least one stable intermediate perch and diagonal connections allow movement between zones without descending to the substrate.

LOWER LEVEL • COOL SIDE cooler / more humid perch

Denser plant and visual cover is useful here. This is a retreat, not an inferior perch.

3. Perches - diameter, length and texture

EmeraldTreeBoas.org notes that adult Corallus usually prefer perches slightly thinner than the widest part of their body. The main perch should extend across most of the enclosure's length so that the whole body is supported in a saddle coil. Additional perches may be shorter and have different diameters.

Riethausen observed an adult female in Guyana on a branch estimated at about 1.5 cm in diameter, and prefers relatively thin, rough perches rather than thick pipes in captivity. This does not mean every adult female must be given a 15 mm perch. The best approach is to offer several diameters and observe the individual animal's choice.

Natural wood

Natural wood offers excellent texture and variable diameter. Sources disagree about some woods: Riethausen uses cherry, among others, whereas EmeraldTreeBoas.org lists cherry among woods of chemical concern. A safer public recommendation is therefore well-identified, clean hardwood with an established history of use, such as maple, oak, birch, beech, willow or magnolia.

PVC / synthetic perches

PVC pipe is durable and easy to disinfect, but a completely smooth surface provides too little friction. If used, its surface should be textured or covered with a safe material that improves grip.

Resin / 3D printing

Textured resin or suitable plastic perches are practical: they do not rot, can have a natural texture and are easy to wash. Modular 3D-printed mounts make it easier to alter height without rebuilding the enclosure.

No compromise: no perch may rotate, bend significantly or rely solely on suction cups. Suction cups gradually lose adhesion in humid conditions and are not appropriate mounts for a main perch.

4. Vegetation and visual cover

Riethausen mainly uses sturdy artificial plants because they are easy to disinfect. EmeraldTreeBoas.org, by contrast, strongly emphasises the role of living vegetation in stabilising humidity, creating a light gradient and, above all, providing security.

Upper and middle levelsPothos, philodendron, Cissus, Hoya and Ficus pumila. Climbers can connect perches but should not obstruct airflow.
Lower level / cool zoneFerns, peperomias, prayer plants and other broad-leaved plants. The cool end can have denser cover.
EpiphytesBromeliads, Tillandsia and selected orchids create a natural upper layer, but must be secured so they cannot fall under the animal's weight.

Plants from a garden centre should not go straight into the enclosure. EmeraldTreeBoas.org recommends removing the production soil, rinsing roots thoroughly, repotting into clean fertiliser-free substrate and waiting 4–8 weeks to reduce the risk of systemic pesticide residues. Avoid plants with irritating sap, particularly Euphorbia.

5. Hides - visual concealment is especially important for Corallus

The main priority is for the snake to feel concealed on its perch. Vegetation, cork panels and carefully positioned leaves should create partly open zones in which the animal can still thermoregulate. The densest cover may be on the cooler side; the warm end should not be so overgrown that it blocks heat, UVB or ventilation.

6. Substrate and floor

Riethausen uses about 5 cm of coconut fibre for adults and disposable liners or paper towels for juveniles, primarily because these make even small amounts of urine and faeces easier to monitor. Both approaches are reasonable. Naturalistic substrate increases the humidity buffer and cushions a possible fall; a liner offers maximum hygiene control. Neither system should develop a persistently wet, oxygen-depleted floor.

7. Ventilation - design it together with humidity

Cross-ventilation works best: for example, a low inlet on one side and a higher outlet opposite. Rising warm air assists natural circulation. EmeraldTreeBoas.org stresses that low humidity should not simply be addressed by closing all vents, because stagnant, humid air increases microbial load.

8. An example layout for an adult Corallus

70–85% of enclosure heightmain warm perch

These percentages are a practical Gonzo Exotics design scheme, not mandatory values from a publication. Final perch heights are determined using actual temperature and UVI measurements, behaviour and the individual animal's size.

A professional Corallus enclosure check

✓ at least three functional perch levels ✓ the main perch supports the entire coiled body ✓ the cool end provides cover, not just a lower temperature ✓ the snake can move at height without going to the floor ✓ ventilation works while the humidity cycle is maintained ✓ branches, mounts and plants support the animal's full weight

Temperature, humidity, misting and drying - husbandry practice

In V8, we use a conservative programme based mainly on Jörg Riethausen's practice and comparison with other specialist sources. We do not recommend a routine night temperature of 20°C. Riethausen describes keeping his animals at around 25°C at night and a maximum of about 32°C during the day, with the gradient and the ability to choose perches at different temperatures being the key considerations.

Day - warm zoneabout 29–31°C, with a local maximum near 32°C, not throughout the enclosure.
Day - cooler zoneabout 25–27°C, depending on enclosure height and construction.
Night - working range24.5–26°C, aiming for about 25°C in the main resting area.
Baseline humidityRiethausen: usually around 45–70% RH; other sources use higher values. Airflow is crucial.

Why do we not maintain a constant 90% RH?

Riethausen notes that, while observing wild Corallus in Guyana, humidity at a particular site was below 50%. An enclosure also lacks the intense air exchange of a forest. High temperature, high humidity and organic debris together favour bacteria, fungi and biofilm.

A practical daily cycle for adults

Evening

A short misting cycle after lights-out. RH may rise to about 75–90%. Misting should create a temporary humidity pulse and drinking droplets, not soak the entire enclosure for hours.

Night

Humidity remains higher after misting but should gradually decline overnight. A working temperature of about 25°C.

Morning

Surfaces may still be slightly damp, but drying should be evident. Additional morning misting is used only when the animal genuinely needs more hydration.

Day

The drying phase: most surfaces dry, with RH often around 45–60%, while fresh water remains continuously available and ventilation is maintained.

Shedding period

Riethausen describes raising RH to about 90% for a few days before shedding. This is not his year-round baseline. After the shed, he returns to the normal cycle.

Different husbandry approaches

EmeraldTreeBoas.org gives higher values, typically 75–90% RH. One approach should not be presented as the only possible method. Differences arise from ventilation, enclosure size, temperature, substrate and misting methods, among other factors. Gonzo Exotics emphasises what sound systems share: stable temperatures, access to water, substantial air exchange and no permanently wet microclimate.

Monitoring: measure temperature on several perches. Riethausen notes that in a tall enclosure in a cool room, the head of a snake hanging in a hunting posture may be several degrees cooler than its body. This is why the profile of the entire enclosure matters.
FEEDING • HYDRATION • BODY CONDITION

Feeding Corallus caninus - a detailed guide

The Northern ETB is an energy-efficient ambush predator. The most common mistake is offering prey that is too large, too often. An adult should be compact and muscular, not weighed down by fat.

Its natural diet includes small vertebrates, particularly mammals. Birds may be taken, but the stereotype of a bird specialist is an oversimplification.

NEONATEsmall prey

After the first shed, often about every 5–7 days if digestion is normal.

JUVENILE7–10 days

Do not accelerate growth.

SUBADULT10–14 days

The interval increases with prey size.

ADULT14–21+ days

Adjust to body condition and activity.

Prey size

The safest choice is prey with a diameter similar to or slightly smaller than the widest part of the snake's body. A huge bulge is not the aim. A smaller meal is easier to digest and makes energy intake easier to control.

Frozen/thawed

Preferred for an established animal because it eliminates the risk of bites from live rodents. Thaw prey completely and warm it moderately; functioning heat-sensing organs usually do not require aggressive teasing.

  1. Feed after dark.
  2. Use long feeding tongs.
  3. Do not force prey into the mouth.
  4. Once the snake takes the prey, allow it to complete a full coil around it.
  5. After feeding, provide undisturbed rest and a full thermal gradient.

Hydration

Hydration is part of the feeding programme. Water should be readily accessible, and the misting cycle should allow the snake to drink droplets. Stabilise a dehydrated snake first — a large meal is not a way to ‘fix’ the problem.

Food refusal and regurgitation

Food refusal may accompany shedding and reproduction. Weight loss, weakening grip, respiratory problems or regurgitation require investigation. After regurgitation, do not offer another meal the next day.

Breeding female

Do not power-feed before the season. Appetite may disappear during gestation. Resume feeding gradually after birth, and base any decision about another season on the female's return to full condition.

Record: the date, prey item, refusals, faeces, sheds, weight and regurgitation. A trend over several months is far more reliable than judging ‘by eye’.
Basis: Jörg Riethausen's husbandry practice and the natural-history literature on Northern Corallus caninus.
BREEDING GUIDE • CORALLUS CANINUS

Breeding Corallus caninus — from preparing the pair to birth

Corallus caninus is live-bearing, and reproduction places demands on the female for much longer than in egg-laying species. Zoological accounts generally indicate maturity at 4–5 years in females and 3–4 years in males, with gestation lasting about 6–7 months. Females in the wild and in captivity often do not reproduce annually. In practice, a reasonable standard is at least one full recovery season after giving birth.

An important taxonomic distinction: many classic husbandry articles labelled ‘Corallus caninus’ concern the Amazon Basin Emerald Tree Boa, now most often classified as Corallus batesii. Steve Volk's figures — including 800 g for a male, 1500 g for a female and night-time drops to around 22°C — are therefore valuable experience with a closely related taxon, rather than a mandatory protocol for Northern Corallus caninus.

1. Assessing a pair for breeding

FEMALE safest at ≥ 4.5–5 years

For Northern Corallus caninus, there is no single scientifically well-established ‘minimum breeding weight’ applicable to every locality and line. The priorities are therefore a fully adult build, stable weight, strong musculature and adequate energy reserves without obesity.

The commonly quoted threshold of around 1500 g comes mainly from Amazon Basin / C. batesii practice. Do not feed a smaller Northern female excessively to force her to reach that figure.

MALE usually ≥ 3–4 years

A male should be fully grown for his line, active at night and in good condition. The approximately 800 g quoted in husbandry literature applies primarily to Amazon Basin animals; for Northern animals, maturity and a stable body condition matter more than the number on the scales.

Do not pair a female if:

  • she is still growing rapidly and has not developed a stable adult build;
  • she has recently completed a pregnancy or has not regained weight and muscle tone;
  • she has recurring dehydration, oral, respiratory or shedding problems;
  • she is obese — excess energy reserves do not replace muscular fitness and physiological capacity;
  • the condition of her reproductive tract is uncertain following earlier complications.
Best practice: arrange a complete assessment of the female before the breeding season, including ultrasound for valuable animals or those with a history of reproductive problems. In live-bearing Corallus, preventing a problem is much easier than responding to dystocia at the end of a pregnancy lasting many months.

2. An annual rhythm — not a ‘permanent rainy season’

Information on the precise timing of C. caninus reproduction in the wild is limited and not always consistent, although zoological observations confirm seasonality. In captivity, it is better to use a gentle annual rhythm than abrupt changes. Jörg Riethausen emphasises a stable gradient; his standard husbandry uses nights around 25°C and distinctly warmer days.

APRIL–JULYstable husbandry / recovery

A photoperiod of about 12 h. Nights around 25–26°C. A normal humidity cycle of 45–70%, rising briefly after misting. Recovery is the main priority for females that have given birth.

AUGUST–SEPTEMBERcondition assessment

Weighing, records, and checks of hydration and faeces. Do not ‘load up’ the female with food before the season.

OCTOBERstart of seasonal changes

Gradually shorten lighting from 12 h to around 11–11.5 h. Night temperatures may be gently reduced to around 24.5–25°C.

NOVEMBER–JANUARYpairing window

A photoperiod of about 11 h. Nights around 24–25°C, with full access to a warm perch during the day. Evening misting may be slightly more intensive, but the daytime remains the drying phase.

JANUARY–MARCHovulation / POS

Stop pairing after clear ovulation. Following the post-ovulation shed, move the female into stable gestation conditions.

SPRING–SUMMERgestation / birth

The timing of birth varies. Zoological accounts generally indicate about 6–7 months of gestation in C. caninus.

3. Breeding-season parameters

Day — warm percharound 29–31°C, with access to a cooler area.
Night — seasonal standardaround 24–25°C as a conservative range for Northern animals.
Photoperiodaround 11–12 h, adjusted gradually.
RHa baseline around 45–70%, temporarily higher after evening misting.

Steve Volk used night-time drops as low as around 22°C for Amazon Basin animals. This range must not be applied automatically to Northern C. caninus. If a pair does not respond to gentle seasonal conditioning, the first step is to assess maturity, condition, male fertility and actual measurements, rather than reduce temperatures further.

4. Humidity during pairing

AFTER LIGHTS OUTa rainfall pulse

Brief misting may raise RH to around 75–85%, providing drinking droplets and an environmental cue.

NIGHThigher humidity + ventilation

Do not close ventilation just to ‘maintain 90%’.

DAYclear drying phase

Most surfaces should dry, with RH often returning to around 45–60%.

Constant 90% RH is not necessary during copulation. Hydration and a predictable daily rhythm matter most. Persistently wet perches with poor ventilation do not increase ‘fertility’; they increase the microbial burden.

5. Pairing Corallus caninus — step by step

  1. Pair them in the female's enclosure. This is the most commonly used arrangement in Emerald Tree Boa breeding.
  2. Move the male during the day. This reduces the risk of a feeding-response strike.
  3. Supervise the first hour. Intense movement by the male, scent investigation and changes of position are normal. Biting and actual fighting require immediate separation.
  4. Never introduce two active males to one female. Serious male combat can occur in Emerald Tree Boas.
  5. If the pair remains calm, leave them together for 5–7 days. Copulation usually takes place at night and may last many hours.
  6. Separate them for several days. This allows time for rest, hydration and, if appropriate, feeding outside the shared enclosure.
  7. Repeat the cycle for several weeks if courtship continues and the female has not yet shown clear ovulation.

Observe without disturbing

An infrared camera is extremely useful. It allows actual copulations to be confirmed without opening the enclosure at night. Two snakes sharing a perch are not evidence of successful mating.

6. Ovulation and when to stop pairing

After several weeks of successful copulation, the female may develop a large mid-body swelling resembling a very large meal. In Emerald Tree Boa breeding practice, this appearance may last around 12–24 hours. Steve Volk described a post-ovulation shed around 30 days after ovulation in Amazon Basin animals. For Northern animals, treat this as a useful reference point, not a clock.

After clear ovulation, remove the male and return to more stable nights around 25–26°C. There is no benefit in leaving the male with a gravid female for many weeks.

7. Managing a gravid female

TEMPERATUREstable gradient

Provide a warm perch around 29–31°C and cooler areas. The female regulates her own basking time.

NIGHTaround 25–26°C

Do not continue pronounced cooling after seasonal conditioning ends.

HYDRATIONa priority

Fresh water daily/regularly, with opportunities to drink droplets after misting. A gravid female must not be dehydrated.

FEEDINGwithout forcing

Many gravid females refuse food. Do not try to ‘push’ large meals.

Steve Volk emphasised hydration as a critical part of Emerald Tree Boa gestation. This does not mean a rain chamber is necessary. If the female drinks, is properly hydrated and the enclosure has a good humidity cycle, stability matters more than additional intervention.

8. When should birth be expected?

Corallus caninus gives birth to live young. General zoological accounts indicate about 6–7 months of gestation and most often 5–12 young, although litters may be larger. For Amazon Basin animals, Steve Volk reported around 130–180 days from the post-ovulation shed. These ranges show why a single ‘due date’ should not be calculated from the first copulation.

9. The final weeks

  • keep handling of the female to the absolute minimum;
  • check the stability of every perch — a gravid female is considerably heavier;
  • maintain access to several temperatures, rather than one ‘hot shelf’;
  • provide fresh water very close to the preferred perch;
  • prepare neonatal containers before the expected birth period;
  • do not increase humidity to a constant 90–100% while waiting for birth.

10. Birth and neonates

Move neonates into separate, easily monitored containers with thin perches. Offer the first meal after the first shed. Northern C. caninus often start feeding more readily than Amazon Basin animals, but every neonate needs individual assessment.

11. The female's recovery after birth

After birth, the female needs stable conditions, water and a gradual return to feeding. Do not plan another breeding attempt the following season simply because she resumes feeding quickly. In this species, prolonged gestation and mobilisation of reserves justify at least a season off.

Warning signs

Persistent straining without birth, unusual swelling, foul-smelling cloacal discharge, weakening grip, rapid loss of condition, open-mouth breathing or prolonged time on the enclosure floor require urgent consultation with a reptile veterinarian. In live-bearing Corallus, a delayed response to dystocia may endanger the female and her young.

HEALTH • PREVENTION • DIAGNOSTICS

Corallus caninus — a complete guide to health and disease

Important: this section is a prevention and diagnostic guide for keepers. Antibacterial, antiparasitic and analgesic drugs, sedation and surgery should be selected by a reptile veterinarian after examining the animal. In snakes, the same sign may have environmental, bacterial, parasitic or viral causes, so treatment without a diagnosis often delays identification of the actual problem.

1. Respiratory disease

Respiratory signs in Corallus caninus should be taken seriously, as snakes may conceal deterioration for a long time. Causes include bacteria, fungi, parasites, viruses and environmental factors. Constant high humidity with poor ventilation increases the risk of problems, but simply ‘lowering humidity’ does not treat established pneumonia.

  • mucus/foam in the mouth;
  • open-mouth breathing;
  • wheezing and increased respiratory effort;
  • holding the head high for prolonged periods without typical hunting behaviour;
  • weakening grip and falling from perches.

Investigation of more serious cases should include assessment of the lower respiratory tract; depending on the signs, radiographs, endoscopy/lavage, cytology and culture may be appropriate. Empirical antibiotic treatment may miss the pathogen and make subsequent culture more difficult.

2. Serpentoviruses / nidoviruses

The strongest association between serpentoviruses and severe disease has been described in pythons, but viruses in this group have also been detected in boas and other snakes. For Corallus caninus with respiratory disease in a large collection, discuss PCR as part of the differential diagnosis with a veterinarian, especially if pythons are housed in the same facility.

There is no specific, established antiviral treatment. Isolate suspected animals, interpret PCR results alongside clinical findings and repeat testing if appropriate.

3. BIBD / reptarenaviruses — particularly important in boas

Boid Inclusion Body Disease is associated with reptarenaviruses and is one of the most serious infectious threats in Boidae collections. Boas may remain asymptomatic carriers for a long time. Signs include regurgitation, weight loss, stomatitis, secondary pneumonia and neurological abnormalities.

There is no effective treatment targeting the cause of BIBD. Prevention relies on quarantine, hygiene between animals and appropriate laboratory testing before a new boa joins a breeding collection.

4. Stomatitis

Early oral inflammation may look minor: small petechiae, reddened gums or a single swelling. Advanced cases produce caseous discharge, necrosis and an unpleasant smell. In arboreal boas, repeated strikes against glass or mesh can also cause trauma.

Treatment includes removal of dead tissue, microbiological investigation where appropriate, antibiotics selected by a veterinarian, and correction of temperature, ventilation and stress.

5. Ophionyssus natricis mites

Mites may hide beneath scales, around the eyes and in enclosure crevices. Signs include excessive activity, more frequent soaking, irritability, rough skin, retained shed and, in heavy infestations, anaemia.

Isolation

Treat an infestation as potentially affecting the entire room.

Monitoring enclosure

Paper + simple perches + no porous decorations until eradication.

Treatment

A veterinarian should select the product; recent publications describe the effectiveness of isoxazolines.

Repeated control

Control must also address the environment and successive parasite stages.

6. Shedding problems

Abnormal shedding may result from dehydration, mites, systemic disease, unsuitable ventilation or a lack of good rubbing surfaces. In Corallus, maintaining 90–100% RH around the clock is not the answer to every problem. First assess the humidity cycle, drinking and health.

Do not lift retained spectacles with tweezers. Repeated retention of eyecaps requires investigation for mites, hydration problems and systemic disease.

7. Dermatitis, blisters, necrosis and burns

Constant skin contact with wet, dirty surfaces may lead to dermatitis. Burns can occur from unguarded emitters or panels. First remove the source of injury and use a sterile, easily monitored setup. Extensive lesions, blisters, discharge, deep necrosis or involvement of many scales require veterinary attention.

8. Cloacal / intestinal / reproductive-organ prolapse

Prolapse is an emergency requiring veterinary care. Possible causes include constipation, parasites, diarrhoea, reproductive strain and trauma. Keep the tissue moist and clean until transport, without forcibly pushing it back.

The veterinarian identifies the prolapsed organ, assesses its blood supply, reduces swelling, replaces the tissue or performs surgery, then investigates the cause. Replacement alone, without addressing the cause, favours recurrence.

9. Internal parasites

Faecal testing should be standard for a new animal. Parasites found in snakes include Kalicephalus, Strongyloides, ascarids and the lung parasite Rhabdias. Heavy burdens may cause weight loss, regurgitation, diarrhoea, anaemia or respiratory signs. Treatment is selected for the specific parasite.

10. Cryptosporidium serpentis

Chronic regurgitation several days after feeding, progressive wasting and thickening around the stomach require investigation for gastric cryptosporidiosis. There is currently no reliable treatment that eliminates C. serpentis. A positive animal poses a long-term epidemiological risk to the collection.

11. Dystocia and gestation problems

Corallus is live-bearing, so problems may include retained foetuses, abnormal eggs/ova, reproductive-tract inflammation or dystocia. Abnormal straining, rapidly increasing weakness, persistent unusual swelling or foul-smelling discharge are warning signs. Ultrasound is particularly valuable because it allows assessment of the foetuses and reproductive tract without surgery.

12. Preventive care in a Corallus collection

Quarantine

3–6 months or longer depending on risk; separate space, equipment and servicing order.

Faecal tests

On arrival and for follow-up after treatment.

Virological screening

Reptarenaviruses/BIBD are particularly important in Boidae; serpentovirus testing depends on the collection's risk.

Mites = a collection-wide alert

Do not treat just one enclosure.

Water

Clean frequently; biofilm develops rapidly in warm conditions.

Records

Weight, sheds, faeces, appetite, treatment and reproduction.
