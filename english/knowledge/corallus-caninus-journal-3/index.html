Corallus caninus — first introductions and a change of strategy. Journal #3

The first introductions

The first two attempts to introduce Klara to the male did not produce a clear breakthrough.

There was no aggression, no problems with being together, but I didn't see any strong, clear reproductive interest either. The male remained rather calm, at times very inactive, and on the coldest nights his activity decreased even more.

At that point, instead of cooling further, I decided to pause and reconsider the direction of this season.

And here a very interesting twist appeared.

A conversation with Warren Booth

I had the opportunity to talk directly with Dr. Warren Booth - a long-time boa breeder, genetics and reproductive biology scientist who also has hands-on experience with breeding the Northern Emerald Tree Boa, or Corallus caninus.

His approach to seasonal conditioning in emerald tree boas is distinctly different from traditional protocols based on a pronounced nighttime temperature drop.

After our conversation, I decided to change my strategy and follow his approach more closely for the rest of the season.
The most important change concerns temperature.

Gradually warmer nights

We had previously reached nighttime lows of around 21°C. I am now gradually moving away from that deep cooling and raising the nighttime temperature.

I am not doing this abruptly. The aim is a gradual transition to a warmer, more stable range while observing the animals’ responses.

This is especially important for the male, whose activity clearly decreased at the lowest temperatures.
The second major change concerns how the pair is introduced.

A longer period together

Instead of short introductions lasting one or two nights, I intend to leave the pair together for longer.

Warren Booth introduces his emerald tree boas in autumn and leaves the male with the female for a long period during the breeding season.

He observes most of the mating activity only around March, and ovulation most often occurs in April or May, although he has also observed it much earlier.

Klara and the male will therefore be left together for a much longer period.

Unless ovulation occurs earlier or a situation requires separation, this arrangement may continue until May.

This is a very interesting change for me, because it means moving away from thinking:

"there was no mating for two nights, so something isn't working"

towards:

“Let us give the animals time and stable conditions.”
Another important part of Warren’s approach is humidity.

Humidity, misting and drying

He does not control the season using particular relative humidity readings on a hygrometer.

He told me directly that he does not use a humidity meter.

He normally mists the enclosure in the evening. This increases humidity at night, when the emerald tree boas are most active.

He also mists the substrate.

To simulate a wetter period more strongly, he adds an extra misting in the morning.

In my view, this fits the natural seasonality of equatorial regions, where annual changes in rainfall and food availability can be more pronounced than changes in day length or temperature.

For me, the following cycle remains important:

more humid at night → gradual drying → good ventilation → misting again.

It's not about keeping the terrarium constantly wet.

The aim is a more pronounced “rainy season” signal.
Warren also described a specific feeding routine.

Feeding and separating the pair

He does not feed the snakes while the pair is housed together.

In his system, the boas receive no food from 1 November to 1 March.

Only when he observes repeated copulations in March does he separate the pair.

The female is then offered a larger meal; the male is offered a normal meal if he is willing to eat.

The animals then remain apart for several days before being paired again.

This is important: keeping the pair together for a long period does not mean feeding them together in one enclosure.
The male’s behaviour as we move away from the lowest temperatures will now be especially important.

What I will observe

I will pay attention primarily to:

nighttime activity, exploration of the enclosure, interest in the female, tail contact, the animals’ positions relative to one another and, of course, any confirmed copulations.

I will not treat simply sitting side by side or a nighttime hunting posture as proof of reproductive activity.

Later in the season, gradual swelling around the middle of the female’s body will be a key sign to watch.

Warren noted that this swelling can become increasingly visible as a female approaches ovulation, while ovulation itself produces a particularly pronounced enlargement around the middle of the body.
I am not changing course because the earlier protocol was “wrong.”

Why I am changing direction

Corallus caninus has been, and continues to be, bred successfully under different seasonal-conditioning systems, including those using more pronounced temperature drops.

But husbandry also means observing individual animals.

If a male becomes noticeably less active at very low temperatures, and an experienced breeder has documented a method using more stable temperatures, seasonally increased humidity and an extended period together, that is worth considering.

Especially because I received this information directly from Warren Booth when I asked specifically about temperature, humidity, feeding and management of the pair.

That's why this season will be very interesting now.

What comes next

We are moving from short introductions and pronounced cooling towards more stable conditions, a greater role for humidity and considerably more time for the animals themselves.

Will it produce a result?

I do not know yet.

And that's why I keep this journal.

Not to show only a successful result after the fact, but to document the whole process—including changes of decision, observations and moments when earlier assumptions need to be revised.

Now the most interesting part of the season begins.
