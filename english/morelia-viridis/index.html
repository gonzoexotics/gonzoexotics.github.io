Conservation status, CITES and legal origin

Morelia viridis is currently classified by the IUCN as Least Concern (LC), but remains subject to intensive international trade. The species is listed in CITES Appendix II. For keepers, this means an animal's origin should not be treated as a mere formality: purchase records, breeder details and continuity of provenance matter both legally and for breeding.

For animals described by a locality name, distinguish documented origin from phenotypic resemblance. Appearance alone does not prove that an animal is ‘pure Biak’, ‘Aru’, ‘Sorong’, etc.

In practice: state a locality only when supported by a credible pedigree. Colour and pattern may support an assessment, but cannot replace it.
SPECIES • BIOLOGY • SIZE

Morelia viridis — species description, anatomy and remarkable adaptations

Morelia viridis is one of the most specialised arboreal pythons. A slender but strongly muscled body, prehensile tail, distinct head and heat-sensing pits form a set of adaptations for ambush hunting above ground. A typical adult reaches around 1.2–1.8 m; individuals exceeding 2 m are described in the literature, but this is not the norm.

SIZEusually 1.2–1.8 m

Size and build vary between populations and sexes.

LIFESTYLEarboreal

Adults are primarily nocturnal ambush predators.

RANGENew Guinea + Cape York

Most of the range lies in New Guinea; the Australian population occupies a much smaller area.

LONGEVITYmany years

Well-managed captive individuals may live for around two decades.

The anatomy of an arboreal specialist

The body is not ‘coiled on a branch’ by chance. Symmetrical loops distribute weight across the perch, while the tail remains an effective anchor. During hunting, the front of the body extends into a characteristic S-shaped posture, allowing a very rapid strike.

Ontogenetic colour change

Across the complex, neonates may be bright yellow and, in northern Morelia azurea also brick-red. Red young have not been confirmed in southern M. viridis. As they develop, their colour changes to green. Research by Wilson, Heinsohn and Endler supports an adaptive role: juveniles and adults use different microhabitats, and their respective colours improve camouflage. Colour change does not equate to sexual maturity.

Temperament

The label ‘aggressive’ is too simplistic. This is a reactive ambush predator. Many individuals are calm during the day, whereas adopting a hunting posture at night greatly increases the likelihood of an immediate strike at a warm, moving object. Individual variation is considerable. Do not try to ‘tame’ the snake through frequent handling — predictable care with minimal stress is preferable.

Interesting facts

  • the former name Chondropython viridis left the popular captive-keeping term ‘chondro’;
  • heat-sensing pits help detect warm-blooded prey after dark;
  • juveniles and adults differ not only in colour but also in feeding ecology;
  • Morelia viridis and Corallus caninus are a classic example of convergent evolution — similar environments led distantly related lineages to develop very similar body forms and behaviour.
Basis: field studies by David Wilson and colleagues, and the Animal Diversity Web synthesis.
NATURAL HISTORY • RANGE • ECOLOGY

The distribution and life of Morelia viridis in the wild

The range includes New Guinea, some nearby islands and the north-eastern tip of Australia. Modelling by Wilson and Heinsohn identified at least 200,000 km² of climatically suitable habitat in Papua New Guinea, while the suitable Australian area is many times smaller. The species occurs in tropical lowland and foothill forests, including secondary and regenerating forests; records range from sea level to around 2000 m.

Microhabitat changes with age

Juveniles more often use forest gaps and edges, where their yellow or reddish colour matches a different background and where different prey resources are available. Green adults are more strongly associated with closed forest. ‘Tropical forest’ therefore does not mean one fixed set of temperatures, humidity and light — a wild snake selects its microclimate.

Movement — the myth of the motionless chondro

Telemetry of 27 individuals at Iron Range revealed complex sex- and age-related differences. Adult females maintained stable home ranges averaging around 6.21 ha, whereas males and juveniles did not show similarly stable home ranges. Males move in search of females, among other reasons. Resting on a perch for many hours is normal, but does not eliminate the need for space.

Hunting

This is a sit-and-wait predator. It may use the same ambush site for many days, conserving energy. Adults hunt mainly at night; rodents were important prey in the Australian population studied: Rattus leucopus and Melomys capensis. Juveniles use smaller prey, including lizards and invertebrates.

Localities and provenance

Names such as Biak, Aru, Sorong and Jayapura are extremely useful in captive breeding, but phenotype does not prove locality purity. Modern genetic research has revealed deep population structure in the Green Tree Python complex. Pedigree therefore matters more than ‘it looks like an Aru’.

ENCLOSURE GUIDE • MORELIA VIRIDIS

An enclosure for Morelia viridis — designing from the ground up

A good Green Tree Python enclosure is more than a tall box with one perch. It should function as a three-dimensional microclimate system: the snake needs several stable positions with different temperatures, humidity, light exposure and degrees of cover. For an adult Morelia viridis, ReptiFiles gives a starting point of 90 × 60 × 60 cm (length × depth × height), and identifies options including 120 × 60 × 60 cm and 90 × 60 × 90 cm as improvements. For larger females and active animals, consider a larger enclosure, provided the entire interior is functionally furnished.

1. Which enclosure material is best?

RECOMMENDED

PVC / expanded PVC / HDPE

For Morelia viridis, these are among the most practical materials: they do not absorb moisture, are easy to disinfect, retain heat and humidity well, and allow straightforward installation of perch supports, probes, misting and heating. ReptiFiles explicitly lists several PVC enclosures as long-term options.

Best suited to: breeding, display, and rooms with moderate or low ambient temperatures.

A GOOD ALTERNATIVE

Thoroughly sealed plywood / timber

It insulates well and allows custom dimensions. All surfaces, edges, holes and joints must be completely sealed with a non-toxic, water-resistant coating; aquarium silicone can be used on corners once the materials have fully cured.

Risk: poorly sealed wood swells, grows mould and becomes difficult to disinfect effectively.

POSSIBLE, BUT MORE DEMANDING

Glass

It can work, but provides poorer thermal insulation and loses moisture faster through extensive mesh. Transparent sides increase the animal's visual exposure, so covering the back and sides is usually worthwhile. Stable conditions are harder to maintain in a cool room than in PVC.

Advantage: excellent visibility and straightforward disinfection.

Gonzo Exotics recommendation: for an adult Morelia viridis, I would choose front-opening PVC/HDPE with opaque back and sides, well-planned cross-ventilation and safe mounting options for lighting and heating. This is a design recommendation based on material properties and contemporary guidance, rather than the only method that can work.

2. How should perches be arranged along the gradient?

ReptiFiles emphasises several stable branches, while experienced breeder Branden Darlison-Hoskin describes perch diameters of roughly half to the full width of the snake's body. In practice, offer 2–3 diameters and let the animal choose. Perches must be rigid, without rotating or bending under the snake's weight.

UPPER / WARMER AREA main thermoregulation perch

Position it so the coiled snake's back is safely within range of the heat source and any UVB. Do not set the height ‘by eye’ — measure the temperature on the perch itself and on the animal's back.

MIDDLE LEVEL transition perch + plant cover

A comfortable area and route between warmth and coolness. It should allow movement without descending to the floor.

LOWER / COOLER AREA cooler perch + hunting position

ReptiFiles recommends at least one perch no more than around 30 cm above the substrate as a lower hunting position. This area is usually cooler and more humid.

A practical layout in a 120 × 60 × 60 cm enclosure: one long perch in the upper half on the warmer side, another at a similar or slightly lower height on the cooler side, an additional intermediate perch and a low hunting perch. Diagonal connections or thick vines should allow night-time movement.

3. Which perches should you choose?

Natural branches

These provide the best variation in texture and diameter. They must come from a reliable location free of chemical contamination, be structurally sound, thoroughly cleaned and completely dried before installation.

Synthetic perches

Ridged, plastic-coated garden stakes, textured plastics or suitably prepared pipes are very easy to wash and disinfect. A smooth surface is not ideal — the snake needs a secure grip.

Mounting

Wall-mounted brackets or removable systems that prevent rotation under the animal's weight work best. Being able to remove a perch without struggling with the snake makes hygiene much easier.

4. Hides and visual cover

For an arboreal species, a ‘hide’ need not be a cave on the floor. ReptiFiles includes elevated hides, and dense vegetation can create secure areas without preventing the animal from observing its surroundings. Provide cover on both the warmer and cooler sides, so the snake does not have to choose between security and an appropriate temperature.

5. Vegetation — live or artificial?

Live plants

ReptiFiles lists plants including pothos, ficus, dracaena and schefflera. Live plants increase environmental complexity, create shade and help stabilise humidity. They must be strong enough not to break during the snake's nocturnal activity.

Artificial plants

High-quality artificial vines and foliage work well in systems designed for very easy cleaning. Avoid sharp wires, loose fibres, exposed ends and parts that could be swallowed.

6. Floor and substrate

There are two valid approaches. A naturalistic setup uses tropical substrate to help cushion falls and stabilise humidity. ReptiFiles suggests options including an additive-free soil, coconut fibre and sand mixture, with a layer 5–10 cm deep. The other method — particularly practical in quarantine, for juveniles and where close monitoring is a priority — uses hygienic liners or paper that immediately reveal urine and faeces. In either case, the floor must dry between misting cycles.

7. Ventilation and equipment

  • Ventilation openings: ideally at more than one height, to prevent stagnant air.
  • Thermostat: essential for every active heat source.
  • Minimum monitoring: a temperature probe on the warm perch + another in the cooler area + a hygrometer in the middle.
  • Light sources: mounted so the snake cannot climb onto the fitting or touch a hot component.
  • UVB: set the distance according to actual UVI and mesh type, not solely the centimetres printed on the lamp packaging.

A finished enclosure should pass this test

✓ the snake can move from a warm to a cool perch without descending to the floor ✓ several stable perch diameters are available ✓ covered areas exist on both sides of the gradient ✓ after misting, the interior dries within the daily cycle ✓ no lamp or heating element can be touched directly ✓ every perch and bowl can be removed and cleaned effectively

Temperature, humidity, misting and daytime drying

The main V8 correction: this site does not recommend routine night-time drops to 20–22°C. Some sources describe that range as possible, but experienced breeders also use distinctly warmer nights. At Gonzo Exotics, we adopt a more conservative working range for healthy adults: 24–26°C at night. This is closer to the practice of many keepers and provides a greater safety margin in a typical home enclosure.

Day — warm perch28.5–30°C; locally up to about 30–31°C, without overheating the entire enclosure.
Day — cooler area24–27°C, allowing the animal to thermoregulate effectively.
Night — routine24–26°C. Seasonal breeding reductions should be gradual and controlled.
Working humidityusually 50–70% RH across the cycle, rising briefly after misting, then drying clearly.

A practical daily humidity cycle

The safest approach treats humidity as a cycle, rather than a single constant number. For an adult Morelia viridis in a well-ventilated enclosure, the following working model can be used:

Evening / after lights out

Mist the interior briefly, mainly vegetation, walls and selected furnishings. RH may temporarily rise to around 70–85%. The snake should not remain permanently wet.

Night

Humidity stays higher in the first hours after misting, then gradually falls. Continuous airflow and the absence of condensation lasting all night matter more than any specific number.

Morning

If the enclosure and animal clearly dry too quickly, very light misting can be used. If evening misting maintains proper hydration, additional morning misting is unnecessary.

Day — drying phase

Surfaces should be mostly dry. RH may fall to around 45–55%, and even briefly to around 40% in a strongly ventilated enclosure, provided the animal is well hydrated and has constant access to fresh water.

ReptiFiles gives a broad fluctuation range of around 40–70% RH for this species and stresses that the enclosure must be able to dry after misting. This agrees with the practice of many contemporary keepers: high humidity without ventilation is a greater problem than a brief dry-air phase.

Juveniles

Neonate and juvenile Morelia viridis are more susceptible to dehydration than adults. Their drying phase should be shorter and gentler. Monitor skin turgor, shed quality, regular urination and actual drinking. This does not mean keeping juveniles in a constantly wet container.

Shedding

After the eyes become cloudy and immediately before shedding, humidity can be kept higher, usually around 70–80%, while maintaining ventilation. Return to the normal wet–dry cycle after shedding.

Monitoring: measure temperature at least on the warm perch and in the cooler area, and humidity in the middle of the enclosure. A high reading immediately after misting is not a problem in itself; failure to dry afterwards is.
NUTRITION • METABOLISM • CONDITION

Feeding Morelia viridis — a complete nutrition framework

Morelia viridis is an energy-efficient ambush predator. The aim is not maximum growth rate, but firm musculature, proper digestion and stable condition over many years.

Natural diet

Adults eat mainly small vertebrates, especially mammals; juveniles also take lizards and smaller prey. Birds are possible prey, but the stereotype of a ‘bird specialist’ is an oversimplification.

NEONATEafter the first shed

The first shed often occurs around 5–7 days after hatching. Offer small, proportionate prey after the first shed, if digestion is normal.

JUVENILEmoderate growth

Usually about every 7–10 days.

SUBADULTbody-condition monitoring

Most often about every 10–14 days.

ADULTmaintenance

Often every 14–21 days; a large, inactive animal may need less frequent feeding.

Intervals are a starting point, not a prescription. Prey size, temperature, weight and body condition take precedence over the calendar. Prey diameter should usually be similar to, or slightly smaller than, the snake's widest point; do not aim for a huge bulge.

Frozen/thawed food

This is preferred for an established feeder because it eliminates rodent-bite risk. Prey must be completely thawed and moderately warmed so it can be detected by heat-sensing pits. Do not cook or overheat food.

Technique

  1. Feed after dark, when the adult adopts hunting behaviour.
  2. Use long feeding tongs.
  3. Move the prey sparingly; do not hit the snake's snout with it.
  4. Leave the snake undisturbed once it takes the prey.
  5. Maintain the full thermal gradient after a meal and do not handle the animal.

Overfeeding

An excessively round, soft body cross-section, rapid adult weight gain without increasing length and heavy folds between coils suggest excess energy intake. Do not power-feed a female to ‘make breeding weight’.

Food refusal and regurgitation

Food refusal may be physiological before shedding or during the breeding season. Assess weight trends, hydration and behaviour. After regurgitation, do not immediately offer another meal; check temperature, prey size, stress and hydration. Repeated regurgitation requires veterinary investigation.

Most importantly: proper hydration and temperature are part of nutrition. A large meal during dehydration or at inadequate digestive temperatures increases the risk of problems.
Basis: Wilson and colleagues — diet and ecology of wild Morelia viridis; Animal Diversity Web; conservative husbandry practice.
BREEDING GUIDE • MORELIA VIRIDIS

Breeding Morelia viridis — a complete plan from preparing the pair to raising juveniles

The most important principle: breeding Morelia viridis should follow maturity, appropriate condition and stable husbandry, rather than intensive feeding or aggressive cooling. David Wilson's field data indicate mean maturity around 2.4 years for males and 3.6 years for females in the Iron Range population, but females did not breed every year. In captivity, it is safer to treat these figures as biological information rather than minimum targets.

The Gonzo Exotics safety protocol: in this version of the site, we do not recommend routine night-time drops to 20°C. Documented breeding successes at 20–23°C exist, but these are stronger seasonal-conditioning protocols. For typical home husbandry, the first choice remains a gentler cycle, with good ventilation and a daily return to the full daytime range.

1. Is the snake ready to breed?

FEMALE most often ≥ 4 years

Weight: do not treat a single figure as a ‘magic minimum’. Rico Walder described follicular development beginning in many females once they reached around 750–800 g, and therefore did not recommend pairing below this range. In practice, a safe target is a mature, well-muscled female of around 800–1000+ g, taking locality and body build into account. Biak animals may naturally grow larger than smaller lines.

Do not feed excessively to reach a target weight. A muscular four-year-old female weighing 850 g may be a better candidate than a younger, obese female weighing much more.

MALE usually ≥ 2.5–3 years

Males may be fertile at much lower weights than females; husbandry literature even describes successful mating by a small male of around 270 g. This is not a recommended starting weight, however. Full maturity, stable condition, proper musculature and the absence of progressive weight loss during courtship matter more.

Essential conditions before the season

  • at least several months of stable husbandry without respiratory problems, regurgitation or recurring shedding problems;
  • proper hydration and regular passage of urine/urates;
  • no active parasites or other disease — a pre-season faecal test is worthwhile for animals with uncertain histories;
  • the female has clear musculature but is not ‘round’ with fat; the spine should be neither sharply sunken nor completely buried in fatty tissue;
  • body weight is stable and known from records rather than judged ‘by eye’;
  • origin and locality are documented if the breeding programme aims to preserve line purity.
Professional assessment: ultrasound performed by a veterinarian experienced with reptiles can track follicular development and distinguish a genuine reproductive cycle from weight gain alone. For Morelia viridis, this is much more reliable than guessing from behaviour.

2. Natural seasonality — what are we trying to reproduce?

Field research at Iron Range indicates strong seasonality: estimated egg laying occurred in the late dry season, with hatching at the start of the wet season. In the field, increased adult activity after estimated hatching lasted roughly from the end of the year to March. This does not mean extreme wild night-time minima must be copied in captivity. An enclosure contains much less air and offers a smaller margin for error.

3. An annual breeding plan — an example for Europe

This is a working schedule, not a rigid ‘biological calendar’. If an animal is not ready, skip the entire season.

MARCH–JUNErecovery and stable husbandry

Normal temperatures and humidity. A female recovering from previous reproduction regains weight slowly, without power-feeding. A photoperiod of about 12 h.

JULY–SEPTEMBERbuilding condition

Maintain the normal adult feeding rhythm. Record weight and condition. Do not increase portions simply because you plan to breed.

OCTOBERpreparation

Check thermostats, backup temperature monitoring, ventilation, incubator and the pair's records. Start gently shortening the day from around 12 h to 11–11.5 h if using a seasonal photoperiod.

NOVEMBERgentle seasonal conditioning

Daytime temperature on the warm perch remains around 28.5–29.5°C. Gradually set nights around 24–25°C. Introduce each change over several days, rather than in a single night.

DECEMBER–FEBRUARYpairing window

If both animals are healthy and show courtship activity, begin supervised pairings. Maintain the full daytime gradient and a cyclic humidity pattern.

FEBRUARY–APRILovulation / POS

Stop pairing after clear ovulation. Watch for the post-ovulation/pre-lay shed and prepare a safe egg-laying site.

SPRING–SUMMEReggs and incubation

In Northern Hemisphere collections, many females lay in spring or summer. Timing depends on when the cycle actually began, rather than the calendar date.

4. Seasonal temperatures — a safe starting point

Day28.5–29.5°C on the warm perch; a cooler area remains accessible.
Night — starting pointaround 25–26°C.
Night — seasonalaround 24–25°C as a conservative first stage.
Photoperiodusually 12/12; during seasonal conditioning, lighting may gradually be reduced to around 11–11.5 h.

Rico Walder described successful protocols with nights in the low 70s°F and a slight reduction in daytime temperatures for 4–8 weeks; Branden Darlison-Hoskin described success after gradually reducing temperatures as far as 20°C. This shows that several approaches can work — it does not establish that 20°C is necessary. In V10, we recommend trying a gentler programme first. Lack of courtship is not a reason for further, uncontrolled cooling.

5. Humidity and a ‘rainy season’ during courtship

Do not turn the rainy season into a permanently wet enclosure. Maintain the same safe wet–dry rhythm during the breeding season:

EVENINGbrief misting

RH may temporarily rise to around 70–85%. The main aim is to provide more drinking droplets, not to prolong standing water.

NIGHTmore humid, but with airflow

Air must not stagnate. Avoid condensation covering the entire ceiling and perches for hours.

DAYdrying

The enclosure should dry clearly; RH typically returns to around 45–60%, depending on construction.

6. Pairing — step by step

  1. Pair only after preparation is complete. Both male and female should be active at night, properly hydrated and free of disease signs.
  2. Introduce the male into the female's enclosure during the day. This reduces the risk of mistaking a hand or another snake for food.
  3. Observe the first 30–60 minutes. Intense biting, persistent aggression, panic or escape attempts mean the pairing should end.
  4. If the pair remains calm, leave them together overnight. Courtship usually intensifies after lights out.
  5. A practical cycle: 3–7 days together, then several days apart. Repeat if the pair shows no stress and the female has not yet shown clear ovulation.
  6. Feed separately only. Do not offer food while two snakes occupy the same enclosure.
  7. After confirmed copulations, record the dates. There is no need to keep the pair together continuously for weeks.

What are encouraging signs?

  • increased nocturnal male activity and intensive scent investigation of the female;
  • tail alignment and actual copulation;
  • the female gradually losing interest in food;
  • colour change or dulling in some females;
  • a large, short-lived mid-body swelling — the typical appearance of ovulation.

7. When should the pair be separated?

After clear ovulation, further pairings usually serve no purpose. Return the male to his own enclosure and give the female maximum peace. In published breeding accounts, ovulation usually lasted around 12–24 hours; the next characteristic shed is an important reference point. Rico Walder reports that egg laying often occurs around 2–3 weeks after the pre-lay shed.

8. Preparing the female for egg laying

As the cycle advances, prepare a stable, darkened nest box at a level the female can easily enter from a perch. Rico Walder described a box around 20 × 20 × 30 cm with a 5–8 cm opening, but size must suit the individual female. Dry or only slightly damp material, such as sphagnum, coconut fibre or clean paper, works inside.

First-time females sometimes drop eggs from a perch. The floor should be clean and safe, and immediately before laying there should be no deep, open vessel into which eggs could fall. The female must still have access to fresh water.

9. Egg incubation — artificial incubation

TEMPERATURE30.5–31.0°C

Rico Walder gives 87–88°F, equivalent to around 30.6–31.1°C. Temperatures above about 32.2°C are already dangerous.

AIR HUMIDITY> 90% RH

However, eggs should not be wet or touch free water.

DURATIONusually 50–55 days

At an incubation temperature around 30.5–31°C.

MONITORING2 independent measurements

A thermostat + a separate, calibrated thermometer at egg level.

  1. Run the incubator empty for several days before the season and check temperature distribution.
  2. Mark the top of each egg in pencil after laying; do not rotate it unnecessarily.
  3. Eggs can be incubated above or on moist medium, but their surfaces should not remain wet.
  4. Provide high air humidity while maintaining gas exchange.
  5. Do not allow lid condensation to drip directly onto the eggs.
  6. Towards the end of incubation, provide small perches in the hatching container so newly hatched young can climb immediately.

10. Maternal incubation

Morelia viridis can incubate eggs naturally. This is biologically normal behaviour, but increases the female's energy costs, makes individual eggs harder to monitor and usually prolongs fasting. Artificial incubation is more often chosen where parameter control and prompt female recovery are priorities.

11. After laying — female recovery

After removing the eggs, remove clutch odours and remains, clean the enclosure and restore stable conditions without seasonal cooling. Do not immediately try to ‘rebuild’ the female with very large meals. The first meal should be moderate, with subsequent feeding increased gradually. A female that has lost substantial condition should have a season off.

12. Neonates — the first weeks

  • house each juvenile separately, on thin, secure perches;
  • monitor hydration and daily drying closely;
  • it is safest to wait until the first shed before offering food;
  • first prey items should have a diameter similar to the juvenile's mid-body;
  • do not accelerate rearing through frequent feeding — regular digestion and defecation matter most.

When should the season be stopped?

Stop the season and return to maintenance conditions if respiratory infection, abnormal shedding with dehydration, clear loss of condition, persistent aggression between the pair, inability to provide adequate daytime warmth or failure of temperature control occurs. No copulation is a better outcome than breeding achieved at the expense of health.

HEALTH • PREVENTION • DIAGNOSTICS

Morelia viridis — diseases and health problems from A to Z

Important: this section is a prevention and diagnostic guide for keepers. Antibacterial, antiparasitic and analgesic drugs, sedation and surgery should be selected by a reptile veterinarian after examining the animal. In snakes, the same sign may have environmental, bacterial, parasitic or viral causes, so treatment without a diagnosis often delays identification of the actual problem.

1. Serpentoviruses / nidoviruses — particularly important in pythons

Reptile nidoviruses, now often called serpentoviruses, are important respiratory pathogens in python collections. A causal link with severe respiratory disease has been well documented experimentally in pythons. Morelia viridis is among the species in which distinct viral lineages and severe pneumonia have been described.

SIGNSrespiratory tract + mouth

Wheezing, open-mouth breathing, excess mucus, foamy discharge, swelling around the throat, lethargy, reduced appetite and weight loss; shedding disorders and difficulty maintaining a perch position have also been described.

DIAGNOSTICSqRT-PCR / RT-PCR

Most often oral/choanal or oesophageal swabs; other samples may also be used. A single negative result is not an absolute guarantee — serial sampling is sensible when risk is high.

TREATMENTno specific antiviral drug

Treatment is supportive and addresses hydration, appropriate temperature, oxygen therapy/nebulisation at a veterinary clinic and diagnostically confirmed secondary bacterial infections.

BIOSECURITYcollection isolation

Separate equipment, gloves and clothing, ideally with separate space/ventilation for suspected or positive animals.

In a large German screening study, 41% of tested Green Tree Pythons were PCR-positive; this does not mean a 41% infection prevalence in the global population. The study does demonstrate that an asymptomatic positive result is possible, and some pythons remained positive in serial tests over a long period. Results must therefore be interpreted alongside collection history, signs and repeat testing.

Prevention: do not introduce a new Morelia viridis directly into a shared room. Quarantine should have its own tools, bowl, feeding tongs and gloves, ideally with separate ventilation. In a breeding collection, discuss serpentovirus PCR screening with a veterinarian before pairing.

2. Respiratory disease — ‘RI’ is not one disease

Respiratory signs may result from serpentovirus, bacteria, lung parasites, fungi, aspiration, trauma or inappropriate conditions. Inadequate warmth, a chronically wet enclosure with poor ventilation and stress are not ‘bacteria’, but may contribute to disease becoming apparent.

Warning signs

  • open-mouth breathing unrelated to brief stress;
  • mucus, foam or bubbles in the mouth/at the nostrils;
  • wheezing, clicking and increased respiratory effort;
  • holding the head unusually high;
  • weak perch grip and lethargy.

What does appropriate treatment involve?

A veterinarian should assess the lungs and upper respiratory tract; depending on the case, radiographs, endoscopy, tracheal/lung lavage, cytology and culture may be used. Antibiotic selection should follow the most likely diagnosis and, in severe/chronic cases, preferably samples from the lower respiratory tract. A mouth swab alone may reflect normal flora rather than the cause of pneumonia.

3. Stomatitis / ‘mouth rot’

Infectious stomatitis is usually secondary. Predisposing factors include oral trauma, stress, inappropriate temperature, immunosuppression, viral disease and contamination. Early signs are pinpoint petechiae and reddened gums; more severe cases produce thick mucus, caseous pus, necrosis, an unpleasant smell and sometimes bone involvement.

Treatment: professional oral cleaning, cytology/culture where appropriate, treatment of the underlying cause, hydration and veterinarian-selected antibiotics. Disinfecting the mouth alone without addressing the cause usually leads to recurrence.

4. Ophionyssus natricis mites

The snake mite is a blood-feeding ectoparasite. It often gathers around the eyes, beneath scales and in skin folds. It causes restlessness, more frequent soaking, rough skin and dysecdysis; severe infestations cause anaemia and weakness.

1. Isolate

Immediately treat the problem as affecting the entire collection, rather than one enclosure.

2. Simplify the enclosure

Remove porous furnishings and use paper/a liner with easy-to-clean perches.

3. Treat the animal

Recent studies describe the effectiveness of certain isoxazolines, including afoxolaner and fluralaner, but a veterinarian should select the product and dose.

4. Environment

Thorough cleaning, washing crevices, replacing substrate and repeating procedures in line with the parasite's life cycle are essential.

Do not use fipronil, permethrin or ivermectin arbitrarily without a veterinary protocol. Toxicity and large differences in safety between species and products have been described in reptiles.

5. Dysecdysis — abnormal shedding

Dysecdysis is a sign, not a diagnosis. The MSD Veterinary Manual lists causes including inappropriate humidity, skin parasites, infectious disease, deficiencies and a lack of suitable rubbing surfaces. For Morelia viridis, also assess hydration and the ability to move freely across stable perches.

  • check actual humidity, rather than misting-system settings alone;
  • check for mites, especially around the eyes and chin;
  • do not pull off retained eyecaps while dry;
  • if the problem recurs, investigate systemic causes, including respiratory/viral disease.

6. Skin problems, dermatitis and burns

Chronically wet, dirty substrate and poor ventilation favour bacterial/fungal dermatitis. Burns occur when an animal can reach an unguarded lamp, panel or hot surface. Any lesion involving blistering, necrosis, swelling, discharge or extensive colour change requires consultation.

First response: remove the cause, move the snake to a sterile, simple enclosure on paper, maintain an appropriate gradient and seek advice on the extent of the injury. Do not apply random human ointments.

7. Cloacal prolapse

Various tissues may protrude through the cloacal opening — the cloaca, distal intestine or parts of the reproductive tract. Causes may include constipation, parasites, diarrhoea, reproductive strain, trauma or systemic disease.

This is urgent. Tissues quickly dry, swell and may become necrotic. Until the appointment, keep them clean, moist and protected from substrate. Do not force them back. After assessing tissue viability, the veterinarian may reduce swelling, replace the prolapse, place temporary sutures narrowing the cloacal opening or operate, while treating the cause.

8. Gastrointestinal and lung parasites

Snakes may carry nematodes including Kalicephalus, Strongyloides, Rhabdias, as well as protozoa. Some parasites cause regurgitation, weight loss, enteritis or respiratory signs. Treatment should follow faecal examination, a smear or, where lung parasites are suspected, respiratory investigation.

For a new animal, perform at least a faecal examination and repeat it after treatment if abnormalities are found. Not every ‘worm’ in faeces is a snake parasite — it may be a pseudoparasite originating from prey.

9. Cryptosporidium serpentis

This is a serious gastric disease of snakes. Typical signs include regurgitation 1–3 days after feeding, thickening around the stomach and progressive wasting. Texas A&M VM Diagnostic Laboratory emphasises that there is currently no effective, reliable treatment that eliminates infection. Diagnosis may include PCR and examination of faecal/gastric material at an appropriate laboratory.

Do not equate every regurgitation with crypto. Oversized prey, inappropriate temperature, stress and many other diseases produce similar signs. Repeated regurgitation nevertheless requires investigation rather than further feeding attempts.

10. IBD / reptarenaviruses

Boid Inclusion Body Disease primarily affects boas and pythons and is associated with reptarenaviruses. In pythons, it may progress rapidly and cause neurological signs: disorientation, impaired righting responses, stargazing, poor coordination and loss of normal striking and coiling ability, as well as regurgitation, stomatitis and pneumonia.

There is no effective treatment targeting the cause of BIBD. Suspected cases require immediate isolation and investigation. Prognosis is very poor in confirmed clinical disease.

11. Reproductive problems

Females may develop pre-ovulatory follicular stasis, post-ovulatory dystocia, salpingitis or ectopic eggs. Signs include persistent swelling, lethargy, abnormal straining, weakness and failure to lay within the expected period. Radiographs and ultrasound are basic tools for differential diagnosis. Oxytocin is not a drug to give ‘just in case’ — it may be contraindicated when a mechanical obstruction is present.

12. Collection prevention — a system that prevents most disasters

Quarantine for 3–6 months

Purdue recommends 3–6 months for IBD; in a large collection, longer, risk-based quarantine is worth considering.

Separate tools

Feeding tongs, bowls, hooks and gloves labelled for quarantine.

Servicing order

Healthy collection first, then quarantine/suspected animals; never the reverse.

Testing

Faecal examination + clinical examination; consider serial serpentovirus PCR in python collections, especially before breeding.

Daily monitoring

Breathing, perch position, water, faeces, urates, skin and appetite.

Records

Weight, feeds, sheds, treatment, test results and all breeding contacts.
